As Far as Their Parents Went

Consider a child born in Mediterranean Europe in 1945 to parents who had not gone beyond lower secondary school. The probability that this child would also stop there was about 81%. For a child born into a similar family in 1980, it was about 38%. In the Post-Socialist countries, meanwhile, the probability that a child would end up at the same educational level as the parents did not fall at all. It rose, from roughly 44% for those born in 1940 to 57% for those born in 1985.

Did one part of Europe open up while another closed down? My co-author Oğuz Öztunalı and I studied 34 countries in a paper conditionally accepted at Empirica. The Mediterranean change reflects what happened to children from given family backgrounds. The Post-Socialist rise reflects, almost entirely, a change in who the parents were.

The Mediterranean example follows children from one kind of family. The Post-Socialist figure averages across all families. To see why that distinction matters, we need to know both how far children went and where their parents started.

The Setting and the Data

We use the first eleven rounds of the European Social Survey, from the 2002/03 round through 2023/24. Our sample contains 296,237 people born between 1940 and 1985, all interviewed at age 25 or later, when most people have completed their schooling. Because respondents also report how far their mother and father went, we can follow the link between generations over nearly half a century of births.

We sort education into three levels: low (lower secondary or less), medium (upper secondary or post-secondary non-tertiary) and high (tertiary, including university). We do this because a year of schooling does not mean the same thing in Lisbon as in Helsinki, or in 1950 as in 1990. We use the higher of the two reported parental levels. When only one is available, we use that parent’s level. We then group the countries by geography and shared history into the Mediterranean, the Nordic countries, the Post-Socialist countries and the Rest of Europe.

Our models estimate, for every birth year, the probability that a child of given parents reaches each level. We use three measures:

  • Persistence: the probability that a child reaches the same level as the better-educated parent. It counts the child of a graduate who graduates as well as the child of a low-educated parent who stays there, so high persistence is not always bad news.

  • Upward and downward mobility: the probability that a child goes further than the parent, or not as far.

  • Equality of opportunity: the probability of reaching university for a child of low-educated parents, divided by the same probability for a child of highly educated parents. At one, children from those two backgrounds have the same estimated chance of reaching tertiary education. At one-half, the first group is half as likely to reach it.

Why One Number Is Not Enough

Persistence can change for two reasons. The first is structural: children of given parents may become more or less likely to end up where their parents did. The second is compositional: the mix of parents may change, even if nothing changes for children within each type of family.

The Post-Socialist countries show how the second can work. For those born in 1945, the probability of matching one’s parents was 31% with low-educated parents, 62% with medium-educated parents and 57% with highly educated parents. Suppose these three probabilities never change, but parents gradually move from the first category into the other two. Average persistence must rise, because there are now more children in the categories where matching a parent is common. Only the average has moved.

We estimate each contribution by comparing the 1940–1950 and 1975–1985 birth cohorts.

1. Four Regions, Four Different Stories

In the Mediterranean, persistence falls by 37 percentage points between the early and late cohorts, and 93% of that decline is structural. This was the largest change among the four groups, and it coincided with a late but rapid expansion of secondary and university education from a very low base. Most of the decline had happened by the late 1970s cohorts, after which the series broadly flattens.

In the Post-Socialist countries, persistence rises by about 14 points. The changing mix of parents contributes 15 points, and changes within family types contribute minus one. The change in the mix of parents more than accounts for the observed rise.

The Nordic countries look almost unchanged from a distance, since persistence rises by just 6 points between the two periods. That small number hides two large movements in opposite directions. Changes within family types lowered persistence by about 11 points, while the changing mix of parents, driven by the growing share with a university education, raised it by about 17. The Rest of Europe also combines a structural decline, of 13 points, with a compositional increase of 7. Its average conceals national differences: persistence fell sharply in Ireland and the United Kingdom, but rose in Austria, Germany and Switzerland.

2. The Female Disadvantage Reversed

Mediterranean women born in 1940 had an 88% probability of ending up at their parents’ level, against 79% for men. Since the large majority of Mediterranean parents in our sample were low-educated, this mostly meant that daughters were more likely than sons to remain at the bottom. Across cohorts, the gap narrowed and eventually changed sign. For those born in 1980–1985, women’s persistence is 38% and men’s 44%, while the probability of going further than one’s parents is 56% for women and 49% for men.

For these youngest cohorts, estimated upward mobility is higher for women in all four groups. The reversal in the gender gap in persistence is clearest in the Mediterranean. The Rest of Europe shows a smaller reversal. In the Nordic countries, young women match their parents slightly more often than young men do, yet they also move up more often. Persistence therefore needs to be read together with the direction of movement.

3. More Graduates, No Narrower Gap

Between the early 1940s and early 1980s cohorts, our measure of equality of opportunity rises from 0.22 to 0.61 in the Nordic countries, from 0.05 to 0.32 in the Mediterranean and from 0.15 to 0.43 in the Rest of Europe. In the Post-Socialist countries it stays near 0.16 throughout. A child of low-educated parents remains about one-sixth as likely to reach university as a child of highly educated parents.

Between the 1945 and 1980 cohorts, the probability of a university education rose from 57% to 68% for Post-Socialist children of highly educated parents. For children of low-educated parents it moved from 9% to 11%. By the 1980s cohorts, the Mediterranean, which had started from a far worse position, had overtaken the Post-Socialist countries.

So the two Post-Socialist findings belong together. The rise in persistence does not show that family background became more important. The flat opportunity ratio shows that, for the most disadvantaged children, its role in reaching university barely eased either.

4. The Great Gatsby Curve, with and without the Mediterranean

In research on income, countries with more inequality tend to have less mobility across generations, a pattern known as the Great Gatsby Curve. We find its educational counterpart: across our 34 countries, those with more unequal schooling also have higher persistence, with a correlation of 0.56.

However, the result depends on which countries are included. Without the seven Mediterranean countries, the correlation turns negative, at -0.24, and is too imprecise to distinguish from zero. Yet regional averages are not the whole explanation either, since comparing each country only with its own group’s average yields a positive correlation of 0.51.

A Caveat About the Choice of Measure

Return to the Mediterranean child in the opening. Finishing upper secondary school would take that child beyond a parent who stopped at lower secondary. Children of graduates could still be more likely to reach university. The figures capture both possibilities. The slope linking children’s years of schooling to parental education (converted to years) roughly halves, from 1.19 to 0.59. Yet the rank correlation rises from 0.38 to 0.45: children’s relative educational position becomes somewhat more closely associated with their parents’ position.

A second example concerns the Post-Socialist countries. In the binary model, the parental advantage in the odds of reaching tertiary education narrows when low- and medium-educated parents are combined. The one-sixth figure asks a different question. It compares children from the lowest and highest parental categories and measures their relative chances of reaching tertiary education. These are different comparisons, and a single headline about Post-Socialist inequality would hide one of them. The ordering of the four groups’ trends likewise depends on how the model is specified, although our pooled test clearly rejects that the trends are the same.

What the Evidence Cannot Establish

Three limitations bound what we can claim:

  • This is a map, not an explanation: we document what happened and how the changes decompose, but we do not identify why. The persistence we see in the German-speaking countries, for example, is consistent with research on sorting pupils into tracks at an early age. Our data do not test that explanation.

  • The measurement is coarse: our medium category combines vocational and academic schooling, so we miss some of the sorting that takes place within it. Parental education is also reported from memory, years later. And since we study people born in and citizens of the country where they were interviewed, we say nothing about the mobility of immigrants.

  • The groups are small: each contains between 5 and 14 countries, so we put more weight on the size and consistency of the long-run movements than on sharp statistical thresholds. Nobody born after 1985 is included, so we are silent on the most recent generations.

What It Is Good For

When a country reports a rise in persistence, two questions are worth asking. Did children from a given background become more likely to match their parents? How much did the mix of backgrounds change? We would like to see both answers reported alongside the headline figure.

For tertiary expansion, who takes the new places matters. In three groups, children of low-educated parents gained ground relative to children of graduates. In the Post-Socialist group their relative chances barely changed. Graduate counts alone would have missed that distinction.

Why relative chances improved in some places and barely changed in others remains open. Our estimates are available by country and birth year, so they can be matched with the dates of school reforms, changes in tuition and the age at which pupils are sorted into tracks. Readers can look up their own country on the interactive page that accompanies the paper.
